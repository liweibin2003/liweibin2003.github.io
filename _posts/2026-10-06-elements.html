Beyond the "Click": My Academic Shift

During my undergraduate studies, my major is Network and New Media, which belongs to Journalism and Communication Studies in China, but I think it also can be divided into Digital Communication because of its Internet nature. After many of my communication assignments, I think that this subject often needs us to investigate what is the transmission pathway of news and what is the effects of news like audience’s feeling and the posts’ performance. In abstract or academic terms, this discipline highly focused on how the information flow within the technological Internet, especially network topologies, interactivity, and the transmission pathways of information (Holmes, 2005).

However, I didn’t choose Digital Communication to study in my master’s stage. One of the reasons is that most of the professors I worked closely with have a background in sociology or cultural studies, which allowed me to become familiar with some sociological research methods. In addition to this, I found I wasn’t interested in merely exploring the information pathway, and I really want to discover more and deep in the virtual environment. As a result, I applied for a masters’ degree in Digital Sociology with Data Science pathway from the University of Edinburgh.

More Than Just the Internet: What is Digital Sociology?

Differed from Digital Communication, as far as I am concerned, Digital Sociology is a subject that pays more attention to hidden structural power relationships between a lot of subjects under digital topics. Additionally, Digital Sociology is not simply the study of the Internet because digital technologies have embedded into people’s daily life nowadays, and the digital society is an extension of real society (Deborah Lupton, 2014). Although this subject investigates the question around digital circumstance, it actually reflects real-world problems just like how the digitalization progress has widened the gap between different areas like global north and south, and who controls the code, monopolizing the voice and so on.

💡 Core Distinction: While Communication asks how a message travels, Sociology asks who benefits from the information, and what structural inequalities it hidden.

For example, let us consider a recent trending topic on China social media platforms: the news that Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland were pressing Westminster for greater devolution and autonomy. In the context of Digital Communication, maybe I will research why this news get a high popularity ranking in platform or what is the Chinese netizens’ opinion distribution. But using my Digital Sociological lens, I will think about who or what forms of power allowed and propelled this specific information to become such a popular information feed, especially in such a politically sensitive environment, where information is often limited by security departments. Besides this, maybe I will be attracted by why Chinese netizens is happy to see this news and the first thing that comes to my mind is a Digital Nationalism.

The Key Weapon: The Sociological Imagination

What makes the investigation content of the two disciplines to be different is the Sociological Imagination. Actually, I think this noun is a bit abstract, but it does exist when we switch to our sociological mind. The reason why some communication scholars can research specific questions deeply is that they use this imagination subconsciously and maybe they didn’t realize they are moving closer to sociology.

Sociological imagination is a key insight to see through the surface of digital technology and combine the social structure, power relationship and other key factors. It can not only uncover technological neutrality, making us pay attention to some labour under digital systems, but also can help us break the opinion of separating reality from virtuality. What made a deep impression on me is an essay sent by Dr. Peaks after class, called “AI Workers' Inquiry 2026”. This report reveals that AI has not decreased the work load, but has increased the work. This digital technology converts the coding time to modifying the wrong or unexpected errors without reducing the time of the whole progress. Blessed by Sociology Imagination, UTAW can see through the real situation clearly, not like those stupid bosses only urging you to undertake more and more works.

Let’s come back from the hateful hidden force and continue discussing what is Sociology Imagination. At its core, Mills suggested that the Sociology Imagination is the cognitive ability to grasp the intersection of biography and history within society. In my opinion, it requires us to think about the question not merely blame to ourselves such as individual anxiety or a lack of self-control, but to center on public issues or real situations which are caused by social structure. In the digital age, this critical insight can prevent us attributing a lot of unfortunate problems to our characteristics.

"The sociological imagination enables its possessor to understand the larger historical scene in terms of its meaning for the inner life and the external career of a variety of individuals."
— C. Wright Mills (1959)

Case Study: Decoding Career Anxiety on Social Media

To illustrate this, consider a pervasive phenomenon among my peers recently: the intense career anxiety triggered by social media platforms. A large number of posts, claiming that their writers could earn 15,000 or even 20,000 RMB per month, cause many audiences to blame themselves, feeling worthless, and being regretful that they didn’t work hard enough and plan their career earlier. However, is this really due to personal inadequacy? If we apply Sociology Imagination to this digital topic, we can uncover some reasons that make fresh graduates easy to think so.

First of all, the current global economic environment is frustrating and the job market is fiercely competitive, and the average salaries are significantly lower than the salaries described online. The “survivorship bias” makes only the top 1% willing to present their success, while the silent majority remains invisible. Additionally, maybe lots of posts are fake just for likes and views.

More importantly, from a critical angle, we must ask: why do our fresh graduates often see these specific posts? The answer is the algorithmic mechanism that tends to recommend controversial or highly feeling-triggered content to people to awaken personal and collective strong emotion. Under this circumstance, the platform can convert our attention to their data and profits, and people who were influenced by posts believed that it was their laziness resulting in failure in seeking high-paying careers.

Conclusion: Changing the Lens

Ultimately, my transition from Digital Communication to Digital Sociology is not only just a change in my major, but also a shift in how I view the real and virtual world. In an era where digital platforms increasingly shape our realities, equipped with Sociology Imagination, I think we can take a comprehensive view to confront some digital phenomenon, and we can be more flexible and critical during the digitalization progress by connecting our personal anxieties to broader structural problems.
